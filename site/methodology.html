Residual dispersion in vote-transfer estimation — Dirichlet model

← דף הבית

The model

Our convex-optimization solver produces a transfer matrix M that maps K-prev party shares to K-curr party shares on average. But individual ballots vary around this average — some precincts swing harder than the matrix predicts, others swing softer. We model this residual variation with a Dirichlet distribution: for each ballot b, the K-curr vote proportions are drawn from

yb ~ Dirichlet(α · p̂b),  where  p̂b = (xb · M) / ‖xb · M‖1

The scalar α ("concentration") controls how tightly each ballot clusters around the matrix-predicted proportion. The variance of each component is

Var(ybi) = p̂bi(1 − p̂bi) / (α + 1)

Large α → low noise (every ballot looks like the matrix prediction); small α → high noise (precincts wobble considerably).

Estimating α empirically

Given the recovered matrix M and ballot-level data, we use method-of-moments:

α̂ + 1 = Σb,i p̂bi(1 − p̂bi) / Σb,i (ybi − p̂bi)2

We compute this both pooled across all (ballot, party) cells, and per destination party (column of M), revealing which parties cluster tightly around the matrix vs. which fragment unpredictably across precincts.

Why the "effective sample size" analogy?

If each ballot's voters drew independently from p̂b, the variance would be p(1−p)/N for N voters. Real Israeli ballots have ~300–400 voters but the data shows variance equivalent to only α ≈ 30–120 independent draws — i.e., a 3–14× over-dispersion factor relative to pure multinomial sampling. This excess reflects unmodeled local factors: candidate visits, neighborhood-specific events, single-block voting blocs, etc.

Three regimes emerge:
Ultra-Orthodox (ש״ס, יהדות התורה): α = 30–225 — the tightest communal voting in the country. Rabbinic endorsement effectively dictates every ballot's mix.
Major centrist/right Jewish parties (Likud, יש עתיד, כחול לבן, קדימה): α = 50–160. Moderately consistent; mainstream voters scatter somewhat but the matrix captures most of the signal.
Arab parties (חד״ש, רע״ם, בל״ד, Joint List): α = 4–25. The lowest in the country — not because individual Arab voters are flighty, but because their geographic distribution is bimodal (≈80% support in Arab-majority precincts, near-0% elsewhere). The matrix's single transfer fraction can't capture both regimes, so the residuals are huge.

Implications

α is a useful summary statistic for several purposes:

  • Forward simulation (e.g., K26 scenario): plug α back into a Dirichlet noise model to generate realistic synthetic ballots given a hypothesized matrix.
  • Bootstrap-free CIs: closed-form variance for transfer-matrix coefficients, given α and ballot-size distribution.
  • Diagnostic: low α for a party indicates that the linear-stochastic transfer model is a poor fit for that party — either due to demographic bimodality, structural change, or genuine over-dispersion.
